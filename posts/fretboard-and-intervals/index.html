Fretboard scales and interval ear training

22 October 2026

The guitar neck is a grid. Each fret raises a string by one semitone, and neighbouring strings are a perfect fourth apart (five semitones), except G to B, which is a major third (four). Once you see that grid, a scale stops being a list of note names and becomes a pattern you can move.

Scales on the fretboard
#

Choose a root and a scale. Notes shows note names; Degrees shows each note’s role in the scale: 1 is the root, ♭3 a minor third above it, 5 a perfect fifth. Tap a position to hear it, or play the scale up and back down an octave.

A minor pentatonic A C D E G 1 ♭3 4 5 ♭7

Squares are roots. Tap any position to hear it; scroll sideways for the whole neck. With a keyboard, Tab into the neck, move with the arrow keys and press Enter to play.

A few things to try:

  • Move the root. Change A to C and the whole pattern slides three frets up the neck. The shape stays the same; only its position changes.
  • Find the box. A minor pentatonic between frets 5 and 8 is the shape most guitarists learn first: two notes on every string.
  • Compare major and natural minor. Switch to Degrees and change only the scale: the 3rd, 6th and 7th drop by a semitone, everything else stays.
  • Hear the blues note. The blues scale is minor pentatonic plus one note, the ♭5, which sits between 4 and 5.

Hearing intervals
#

An interval is the distance between two notes, counted in semitones. On one string that is simply the number of frets between them: five frets up is a perfect fourth, seven a perfect fifth, twelve an octave.
